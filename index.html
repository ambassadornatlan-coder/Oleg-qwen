2. Abstract

This report presents a comprehensive investigation into the state estimation of dynamical systems characterized by Markovian switching and discontinuous disturbances, with a specific focus on the application of generalized functions (distributions) and their relevance to aircraft navigation. By simulating a multidisciplinary research team, we critically evaluate the mathematical foundations of Markov jump linear systems (MJLS), the rigorous treatment of impulsive and discontinuous dynamics via Schwartz distributions and impulsive differential equations (IDEs), and modern state-estimation algorithms such as the Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) filter. The investigation reveals that while generalized functions provide a mathematically elegant framework for unifying continuous dynamics, discrete mode switches, and instantaneous state jumps (e.g., sensor faults or abrupt maneuvers), their direct application in nonlinear estimation requires careful handling of distributional products. We identify critical research gaps in combining rigorous impulsive IDE frameworks with suboptimal IMM filters for nonlinear aircraft kinematics, providing a structured roadmap for future aerospace navigation research.

4. Executive Summary

Aircraft navigation systems operate in highly dynamic environments subject to abrupt changes in vehicle dynamics (e.g., severe maneuvers, structural damage) and sensor degradation (e.g., GNSS outages, inertial sensor faults). These phenomena are mathematically modeled as Markov switching and discontinuous disturbances. This report synthesizes current knowledge on estimating the state of such systems using generalized functions.

Key Findings:

  • Mathematical Rigor: Modeling disturbances as Dirac delta distributions (generalized functions) is mathematically valid for linear impulsive systems but becomes highly problematic in nonlinear systems due to the Schwartz impossibility of multiplying distributions. Impulsive Differential Equations (IDEs) provide the correct rigorous framework.
  • Estimation Algorithms: The Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) algorithm remains the industry standard for Markov-switching systems. However, standard IMM assumes continuous dynamics within modes. Adapting IMM to handle distributional/impulsive state jumps requires modified covariance update equations at impulse times.
  • Navigation Applicability: Generalized functions are highly effective for modeling instantaneous sensor faults (outliers) and hard impacts. However, they are less suitable for modeling gradual sensor degradation or continuous turbulence, which are better served by stochastic jump-diffusion or robust filtering techniques.
  • Research Gaps: There is a distinct lack of theoretically proven, computationally feasible nonlinear impulsive IMM filters specifically tailored for the 6-DOF (Degree of Freedom) kinematic models of aircraft.

5. Introduction and Research Objectives

The estimation of the state of dynamical systems in aerospace applications is a cornerstone of modern control theory and navigation. Traditional models assume smooth, continuous dynamics and Gaussian noise. However, aircraft navigation systems frequently encounter abrupt changes: sudden GNSS signal loss, abrupt shifts in aerodynamic coefficients due to flap deployment, or instantaneous biases in inertial measurement units (IMUs). Furthermore, the underlying system may switch between distinct operational modes (e.g., takeoff, cruise, landing, or degraded sensor configurations).

8. Mathematical Preliminaries and Notation

To ensure mathematical rigor, we establish the following notation and definitions.

  • \( x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n \): System state vector.
  • \( r(t) \in \mathcal{S} = \{1, 2, \dots, N\} \): Discrete Markov chain representing the system mode.
  • \( \Pi = [\pi_{ij}] \): Transition rate matrix (generator) for a continuous-time Markov chain, where \( \pi_{ij} \geq 0 \) for \( i \neq j \) and \( \sum_{j} \pi_{ij} = 0 \).
  • \( \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}) \): The space of infinitely differentiable functions with compact support (test functions).
  • \( \mathcal{D}'(\mathbb{R}) \): The space of Schwartz distributions (generalized functions), defined as continuous linear functionals on \( \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}) \).
  • \( \delta(t) \): The Dirac delta distribution, defined by \( \langle \delta, \phi \rangle = \phi(0) \) for all \( \phi \in \mathcal{D} \).
  • \( H(t) \): The Heaviside step function, whose distributional derivative is \( \delta(t) \).

Crucial Distinction: A discontinuous function (like \( H(t) \)) is a locally integrable function that can be embedded in \( \mathcal{D}' \). A generalized function (like \( \delta(t) \)) is a distribution that cannot be represented by a classical function. In navigation, sensor faults are often modeled as jumps in the state (requiring \( \delta(t) \) in the derivative), while mode switches are modeled by the Markov chain \( r(t) \).

9. Markov-Switching Dynamical Systems

Agent 1's analysis confirms that Markov Jump Linear Systems (MJLS) are the standard mathematical framework for systems with abrupt structural changes. The continuous-time dynamics are given by:

\[ \dot{x}(t) = A(r(t))x(t) + B(r(t))u(t) + G(r(t))w(t) \]

where \( w(t) \) is a continuous white noise process. The mode \( r(t) \) evolves according to a continuous-time Markov chain with transition rates \( \pi_{ij} \).

9.1 Observability and Estimation

In aircraft navigation, the mode \( r(t) \) is rarely perfectly observable. For instance, a GNSS receiver might switch between "nominal," "multipath degraded," and "outage" modes. This leads to the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) or Jump-Markov Linear System (JMLS) formulation, where the mode must be estimated jointly with the continuous state \( x(t) \). The exact Bayesian filter for JMLS requires maintaining a Gaussian mixture with an exponentially growing number of components. The Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) algorithm [1, 5] provides a computationally feasible suboptimal solution by merging these components at each time step.

10. Discontinuous Disturbances and Generalized Functions

Agent 2's investigation into generalized functions reveals both the power and the severe mathematical pitfalls of this approach in the context of the Ukrainian research topic.

10.1 The Distributional Approach

Proponents of using generalized functions in navigation argue that discontinuous disturbances (e.g., a sudden sensor bias shift or a physical impact) can be modeled as distributional inputs. A common formal representation is:

\[ d(t) = d_{\text{reg}}(t) + \sum_{k} a_k \delta(t - \tau_k) \]

where \( d_{\text{reg}}(t) \) is a regular (continuous or piecewise continuous) disturbance, and the sum represents impulsive disturbances at times \( \tau_k \) with amplitudes \( a_k \).

10.2 The Mathematical Hazard: Products of Distributions

Agent 4 (Math Verification) identified a critical flaw in naive applications of generalized functions to nonlinear aircraft dynamics. If the system is nonlinear, the dynamics might be written as:

\[ \dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), r(t)) + d(t) \]

If \( d(t) \) contains a Dirac delta, the state \( x(t) \) will experience a jump at \( \tau_k \), meaning \( x(t) \) is discontinuous. Consequently, the term \( f(x(t), r(t)) \) becomes discontinuous. In the theory of Schwartz distributions, the product of two distributions (or a discontinuous function and a distribution) is generally undefined (Schwartz's impossibility theorem). Therefore, the expression \( f(x(t))\delta(t-\tau_k) \) is mathematically meaningless in the standard distributional sense.

10.3 The Rigorous Alternative: Impulsive Differential Equations (IDEs)

To resolve this, the literature (e.g., Samoilenko & Perestyuk [3], Zavalishchin & Sesekin [4]) shifts from distributional forcing terms to Impulsive Differential Equations (IDEs). Instead of putting \( \delta(t) \) in the derivative, the system is defined by a continuous flow interrupted by discrete jump conditions:

\[ \begin{cases} \dot{x}(t) = f(x(t), r(t)) + g(x(t), r(t))u(t), & t \neq \tau_k \\ \Delta x(\tau_k) = x(\tau_k^+) - x(\tau_k^-) = I_k(x(\tau_k^-)), & t = \tau_k \end{cases} \]

This formulation avoids the product of distributions entirely. The "generalized function" aspect is retained in the sense that the solution \( x(t) \) is a discontinuous function (a sum of a continuous function and a step function), and its derivative in the sense of distributions contains Dirac deltas. However, the equations of motion are formulated using classical functions and discrete maps, ensuring mathematical well-posedness.

11. State-Estimation Methods for Switching and Discontinuous Systems

Agent 3 evaluated how modern estimation algorithms handle the IDE and MJLS formulations described above.

11.1 Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) Estimation

The IMM filter [1, 5] is the de facto standard for Markov-switching systems. It runs \( N \) parallel filters (typically Extended Kalman Filters, EKF, for nonlinear aircraft dynamics). At each step, it mixes the state estimates based on the predicted mode probabilities.
Limitation: Standard IMM assumes continuous dynamics within each mode. If an impulsive disturbance occurs, the standard IMM will experience a massive innovation (residual), causing the covariance to inflate and potentially leading to filter divergence or incorrect mode probability assignment.

11.2 Impulsive and Robust Kalman Filtering

To handle discontinuous disturbances, researchers have developed modified filters.
Impulsive Kalman Filter: When an impulse is detected (e.g., via a change-point detection algorithm), the filter applies a discrete state update and covariance correction: \( P(\tau_k^+) = J_k P(\tau_k^-) J_k^T + Q_{\text{imp}} \), where \( J_k \) is the Jacobian of the jump map \( I_k \).
Robust Filtering (H-infinity, Huber): Instead of explicitly modeling the disturbance as an impulse, robust filters bound the influence of large outliers. This is highly effective for GNSS multipath or sudden IMU biases but does not explicitly estimate the jump magnitude.

11.3 Particle Filtering for Jump Systems

For highly nonlinear aircraft kinematics (e.g., aggressive maneuvering) with non-Gaussian impulsive noise, Particle Filters (PF) [6] can approximate the posterior distribution. However, standard particle filters suffer from "particle depletion" when sudden jumps occur, as the particles do not anticipate the impulse. Specialized "jump-markov particle filters" introduce auxiliary variables to handle the discrete jumps.

12. Mathematical Analysis and Verification of Key Formulations

Agent 4 independently verified the mathematical claims regarding the integration of generalized functions into state estimation.

12.1 Covariance Propagation with State Jumps

Consider a linear impulsive system with Markov switching. The state jump at time \( \tau_k \) in mode \( i \) is modeled as \( x(\tau_k^+) = D_i x(\tau_k^-) + \eta_k \), where \( \eta_k \) is impulsive noise.
Verification: The standard continuous-time covariance differential equation \( \dot{P} = A P + P A^T + Q \) is invalid at \( t = \tau_k \). The correct discrete update for the estimation error covariance must be:

\[ P_i(\tau_k^+) = D_i P_i(\tau_k^-) D_i^T + R_{\eta} \]

If this discrete update is omitted, and the filter attempts to propagate the covariance continuously through the impulse, the estimated covariance will be artificially small, leading to overconfidence and eventual filter divergence. This mathematical fact is often overlooked in applied literature that merely adds \( \delta(t) \) to the continuous equations without adjusting the discrete filter mechanics.

12.2 Observability under Markov Switching

For a JMLS to be observable, it is not sufficient for each mode to be individually observable. The system must satisfy a "mode-detectability" condition. If the system stays in an unobservable mode for too long, the state estimate will diverge. In aircraft navigation, this corresponds to prolonged GNSS outages (an unobservable mode for absolute position) where the INS drift cannot be corrected. The mathematical condition requires that the average dwell time in unobservable modes is bounded by the observability Gramian of the observable modes.

13. Applications to Aircraft Navigation

Agent 5 mapped the theoretical frameworks to practical aerospace engineering problems.

13.1 Integrated INS/GNSS Navigation

In integrated navigation, the aircraft state (position, velocity, attitude) and sensor biases are estimated.
Markov Switching: The GNSS receiver can be modeled as a Markov chain with modes: {Nominal, Degraded, Outage}. The IMM filter dynamically adjusts the weight given to GNSS measurements based on the estimated mode probability.
Discontinuous Disturbances: A sudden GNSS multipath error or a "hard" IMU fault (e.g., a stuck gyro) is best modeled as an impulsive disturbance. Using the IDE framework, the filter detects the innovation spike, estimates the jump magnitude, and resets the sensor bias state.

13.2 Fault Detection, Isolation, and Accommodation (FDIA)

Generalized functions provide a natural mathematical language for FDIA. A sensor fault is mathematically an additive impulsive or step disturbance. By formulating the navigation error equations using IDEs, the "jump" parameters can be estimated alongside the navigation state. If the estimated jump exceeds a threshold, a fault is isolated. This is practically superior to simple thresholding of residuals, as it accounts for the covariance of the jump estimate.

13.3 Abrupt Maneuvering

When an aircraft executes a high-g turn, the linearized kinematic model experiences a "disturbance" due to linearization errors. While not a true physical impulse, it acts mathematically as a discontinuous input to the linear filter. The IMM handles this by switching to a "maneuver" mode (e.g., a Coordinate Turn model or a Current Statistical model), effectively treating the linearization error as a mode-dependent process noise.

15. Critical Assessment of the Evidence

Agent 6 conducted an adversarial review of the integrated findings to separate established facts from speculative claims.

15.1 The "Generalized Function" Misconception

Claim: Using generalized functions (distributions) to model discontinuous disturbances in nonlinear navigation equations yields a superior, unified mathematical framework.

Assessment: Partially Invalid / Misleading. While mathematically elegant for linear systems, applying Schwartz distributions directly to nonlinear aircraft kinematic equations leads to undefined products of distributions. The literature that claims to use "generalized functions" for nonlinear navigation often implicitly relies on the IDE framework (discrete jumps) or uses formal, non-rigorous manipulations of the Dirac delta. The rigorous approach is to use IDEs, not raw distributional calculus. Therefore, the superiority of "generalized functions" is largely a notational convenience rather than a fundamental mathematical advantage over IDEs.

15.2 IMM vs. Robust Filtering for Sensor Faults

Claim: IMM is always superior to robust filtering for handling sensor faults in navigation.

Assessment: Context-Dependent. If the fault is abrupt, large, and persistent (e.g., total GNSS loss), IMM with a dedicated "outage" mode is superior because it explicitly models the physics of the fault. However, if the fault is a series of small, frequent outliers (e.g., mild multipath), a robust Huber filter is computationally cheaper and prevents the IMM from unnecessarily switching modes, which can introduce transient errors during the mode probability convergence phase.

15.3 Observability during GNSS Outages

Claim: Markov switching models can maintain accurate navigation during prolonged GNSS outages.

Assessment: False. No estimation algorithm can create observability where none exists. During a GNSS outage, the absolute position and velocity are unobservable from INS alone (due to accelerometer bias). The Markov model will correctly estimate the probability of the "outage" mode as approaching 1.0, but the state estimate will diverge due to INS drift. The mathematical model accurately reflects the physical degradation, but it does not "solve" the navigation problem without external aiding (e.g., vision, barometric, or magnetic sensors).

16. Research Gaps and Potential Contributions

Based on the critical assessment, the following verifiable research gaps are identified:

16.1 Gap 1: Nonlinear Impulsive IMM with Proven Stability

  • Description: While linear impulsive filters are well-understood, and standard IMM is widely used, there is a lack of theoretically proven, computationally feasible nonlinear impulsive IMM filters for 6-DOF aircraft kinematics.
  • Closest Existing Approaches: Standard IMM with inflated process noise; separate change-detection algorithms triggering a filter reset.
  • Why it Matters: Aircraft dynamics are highly nonlinear. Ad-hoc filter resets cause transient spikes in estimation error.
  • Proposed Direction: Develop an Impulsive IMM based on the Unscented Transform or Cubature Kalman Filter, incorporating rigorous Lyapunov-based stability analysis for the impulsive jump maps.

16.2 Gap 2: Distributional Modeling of Aerodynamic Mode Switching

  • Description: Current MJLS models treat aerodynamic changes (e.g., stall, flap deployment) as discrete mode switches. However, the transition is often continuous but highly nonlinear.
  • Proposed Direction: Investigate the use of measure-driven differential equations (a generalization of IDEs) where the mode transition is represented by a singular measure, allowing for a unified treatment of gradual and abrupt aerodynamic changes without the combinatorial explosion of IMM modes.

16.3 Gap 3: Real-Time Implementation of Impulsive Particle Filters

  • Description: Particle filters handle impulsive noise best but are too slow for real-time aircraft navigation.
  • Proposed Direction: Develop a Gaussian-sum approximation of the impulsive particle filter, specifically optimized for GPU or FPGA architectures, to achieve real-time performance for high-dimensional aerospace states.

18. Conclusions

This comprehensive investigation into the state estimation of dynamical systems with Markov switching and discontinuous disturbances yields several definitive conclusions for aircraft navigation applications:

  1. Mathematical Rigor is Paramount: The use of generalized functions (Schwartz distributions) to model discontinuous disturbances is mathematically valid for linear systems but fundamentally flawed if applied naively to nonlinear aircraft dynamics due to the impossibility of multiplying distributions. The rigorous alternative is the framework of Impulsive Differential Equations (IDEs), which models state jumps via discrete maps rather than distributional forcing terms.
  2. Estimation Algorithms Require Adaptation: Standard Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) filters are insufficient for systems with impulsive disturbances. To prevent filter divergence, the covariance propagation equations must be explicitly modified to account for discrete state jumps at impulse times. Impulsive Kalman filters and robust filtering techniques offer practical solutions, each with distinct trade-offs in computational cost and fault-isolation capability.
  3. Practical Navigation Value: Generalized-function and IDE models are highly valuable for Fault Detection, Isolation, and Accommodation (FDIA) in integrated INS/GNSS systems. They provide a mathematically sound basis for estimating the magnitude of sudden sensor faults or physical impacts, surpassing simple residual thresholding.
  4. Future Research Direction: The most promising avenue for original contribution is the development of a theoretically proven, nonlinear impulsive IMM filter tailored for 6-DOF aircraft kinematics. Bridging the gap between rigorous impulsive mathematics and computationally feasible aerospace algorithms remains the primary challenge and opportunity in this field.

Ultimately, while the mathematical elegance of generalized functions provides a unifying language for discontinuous dynamics, its practical success in aircraft navigation depends entirely on translating this language into robust, discrete-time algorithmic implementations that respect the nonlinear realities of flight.

Appendix A: Derivation of the Covariance Jump Condition

For a linear impulsive system where the state undergoes a jump \( x(\tau_k^+) = D x(\tau_k^-) + \eta_k \), the estimation error is \( e(t) = x(t) - \hat{x}(t) \). Assuming the filter applies the same jump map \( \hat{x}(\tau_k^+) = D \hat{x}(\tau_k^-) + \hat{\eta}_k \), the error jump is:

\[ e(\tau_k^+) = D e(\tau_k^-) + (\eta_k - \hat{\eta}_k) \]

The covariance \( P(t) = E[e(t)e(t)^T] \) immediately after the jump is:

\[ P(\tau_k^+) = E[e(\tau_k^+)e(\tau_k^+)^T] = D P(\tau_k^-) D^T + E[(\eta_k - \hat{\eta}_k)(\eta_k - \hat{\eta}_k)^T] \]

If the jump estimate \( \hat{\eta}_k \) is the conditional mean, the cross-terms vanish, yielding \( P(\tau_k^+) = D P(\tau_k^-) D^T + R_{\eta} \), where \( R_{\eta} \) is the covariance of the impulsive noise. This proves that continuous propagation of \( P(t) \) through \( \tau_k \) is mathematically invalid and will result in an underestimate of the true error covariance.
